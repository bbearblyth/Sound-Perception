Pitch and tuning: physics, ears and culture

Twelve perfect fifths should bring you back to where you started. They don't. That tiny gap, the comma, explains why every culture's tuning is a compromise, and why "in tune" depends on who is listening.

1. We hear ratios, not hertz

Pitch is the brain's estimate of how often a sound repeats. Going from 110 to 220 Hz sounds like the same leap as going from 1000 to 2000 Hz: an octave, a doubling. That's why musicians measure intervals in cents, with 1200 to the octave and 100 to each equal-tempered semitone.

The top ruler is in hertz: the A's crowd to the left. The bottom ruler is logarithmic, like your ear: every octave is the same width. The top note of a piano is about 4,186 Hz; above roughly 4–5 kHz, melodies stop sounding like melodies.

Build a tone from its harmonics

Real notes contain partials at 1×, 2×, 3×… the fundamental. Neighbouring partials form the "simple" intervals: 2:1 octave, 3:2 fifth, 4:3 fourth, 5:4 major third. Switch partials on and off, then try removing the first: you still hear 110 Hz (the missing fundamental).

How small a difference can you hear?

You'll hear two tones. Are they the same or different? Many listeners stop noticing at about 5–10 cents (around 1–2.5 Hz at 440 Hz); trained musicians often do better.

Which A is the right A?

A has drifted over the centuries: modern baroque ensembles often use 415 Hz, France fixed 435 Hz in 1859, a 1939 London conference recommended 440 Hz, and many orchestras now tune a little higher. Claims that 432 Hz is "natural" or "healing" have no good evidence behind them.

2. Consonance: beats, roughness and fit

Consonance is notes sounding stable, blended and at rest together. Dissonance is notes sounding tense, rough or unresolved. There are two layers: a sensory one (how smooth or rough the sound is, which is much the same for everyone) and a cultural one (whether a style treats an interval as finished or as needing to move on). Dissonance isn't "bad": it's the tension that gives music its movement.

Beats become roughness

Two pure tones close together interfere and pulse at their difference frequency. Speed the pulsing up and it turns gritty (roughness); widen the gap further and you hear two smooth notes. The curve is the classic Plomp–Levelt roughness shape.

3. The comma: you can't have everything

Pure intervals are fractions built from 2s, 3s and 5s. A power of 3 can never equal a power of 2, so stacking pure fifths (3:2) never lands exactly on an octave. Twelve of them overshoot seven octaves by the Pythagorean comma: 531441 : 524288, about 23.5 cents, a quarter of a semitone. The ring at the top of the page draws exactly this.

A second gap, the syntonic comma (81:80, about 21.5 cents), separates the major third you get from four pure fifths (81:64, 408 cents) from the pure third 5:4 (386 cents).

Every tuning system chooses between three goals, and can have at most two, usually only partly: pure fifths, pure thirds, every key usable. Think of flattening a globe into a map: something always distorts, and you only choose where.

Hear the fifth: just versus equal

A just fifth is 702 cents; the equal-tempered fifth is 700. Listen for the very slow beat in the equal version.

4. Western history: where to hide the comma

  • c. 500 BCEThe Pythagoreans derive intervals from string-length ratios on the monochord.
  • 4th c. BCEAristoxenus argues that the ear, not arithmetic, should judge intervals. Greek theory uses diatonic, chromatic and enharmonic tetrachords.
  • MedievalPythagorean tuning: pure fifths and fourths for organum and chant; thirds are tense.
  • 1523Pietro Aron describes quarter-comma meantone: pure major thirds, a "wolf" fifth, remote keys unusable.
  • 1584Zhu Zaiyu in Ming China calculates equal temperament precisely; Simon Stevin does so around 1605.
  • 1691Werckmeister's well temperaments: every key usable, each with its own colour.
  • 1722Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier Book I. Whether he meant equal temperament is still debated.
  • 19th c.Equal temperament becomes the keyboard norm; piano tuners stretch octaves to suit stiff strings.

6. Is any of it universal?

Three studies make a strong closing argument: physics sets the raw material, biology sets some limits, and culture decides what sounds "right".

Equal

Consonance preference is learned

The Tsimane', an Amazonian society with little exposure to Western music, rated consonant and dissonant chords as equally pleasant, though they could hear the difference and disliked roughness (McDermott et al., 2016).

~4 kHz

Some limits are shared, octaves aren't

Tsimane' and US listeners both heard intervals on a log scale and lost melodic pitch above about 4 kHz. But only US listeners sang back high notes as the "same" note an octave lower (Jacoby et al., 2019).

Timbre

The instrument shapes the tuning

Changing an instrument's partials changes which intervals sound consonant. Inharmonic gongs like the gamelan's bonang produce consonances that match the slendro scale (Marjieh, Harrison et al., 2024).

Hear timbre decide the octave

With normal harmonic partials, a 2:1 octave is smooth and a stretched 2.1:1 "octave" beats. Give the notes stretched partials and the result flips. Piano stretch tuning and gamelan tuning both follow from this.

Discuss

If Balinese gamelan players deliberately tune paired instruments apart to make them beat (the shimmering ombak), is "out of tune" meaningful outside a particular tradition? The Tsimane' could hear dissonance but didn't mind it. What's the difference between perception and preference?

7. Microtonality: divide the octave your way

Composers and producers keep trying new divisions: Harry Partch's 43-note just scale, 31-tone equal temperament (proposed by Huygens), the quarter-tone guitars on King Gizzard & the Lizard Wizard's Flying Microtonal Banana. Tools such as Scala files, ODDSound MTS-ESP, Logic's alternative tunings and Ableton Live 12's tuning systems make it easy to try.
